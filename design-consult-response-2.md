# §5 — Answers

## 5.1 Diagnosis: why dispersion wins

**The pattern first, because it is the diagnosis.** Two structural fixes have each produced a new ~70% dominant. That is not two separate balance failures — it is one failure observed twice. The game's placement value is, in every era so far, *monotone along a single axis*: how concentrated your force is. Before twin nodes, the gradient pointed toward concentration, and the harness found the endpoint of that gradient (stack, 70.7%). Twin nodes plus craters flipped the *sign* of the gradient without adding any *curvature*, so the harness found the opposite endpoint (front line, 71.0%). The recurring ~70% number is exactly what "endpoint of a monotone axis, measured against a field of interior points" looks like. Any third fix that flips the sign again — a new counter-weapon included — will produce a third ~70% endpoint. The work is not to flip the gradient. It is to bend it, so the optimum is interior and, crucially, opponent-dependent.

**Why the gradient currently points at dispersion — four stacked mechanisms:**

1. **Coverage economics are concave everywhere.** In a game where nothing moves, a lane you occupy and the opponent doesn't is won at t=0 and stays won for 45 seconds — the first unit into an empty column has enormous marginal value. The second unit in the same column shares targets, shares crater blockage, queues behind the same sandbags, and exposes itself to the only two density taxes in the game (AT pierce, mortar splash). With roughly ten combat units and eight columns, you can cover the width barely once; every doubled column is an uncovered column somewhere else. Marginal value strictly decreases in local density and nothing anywhere increases in it. That is a Colonel Blotto game with concave lane payoffs, and the equilibrium of that game is uniform allocation. Front line isn't an archetype that happens to win; it is the closed-form solution to the current payoff function.

2. **Forward placement is strictly dominant on the depth axis.** `Useful rows = range − gap` means the front rank is the only row where the whole army shoots from t=0. Depth buys survivability only against fire that can reach you, and mostly it buys idleness. So the second half of "front line" — the *front* half — is pure range geometry. Both axes (spread, forwardness) have monotone optima. The effective decision space is currently two dials, both pegged.

3. **The mortar makes objective progress formation-independent.** This is the piece I under-weighted last time. Mortar delivers 63.9% of node damage, from anywhere, over craters, regardless of formation — and that share spiked precisely when node HP halved to 100. So no shape ever needs to *break through* to score. Sandbags at 90 HP do create a genuine convexity — one soldier can't crack a bag inside the cap, two or three can — but that threshold never binds, because the mortar renders breakthroughs optional. Strip the mortar's node damage out and the win paths (node kill, node-damage tiebreak) would have to flow through lanes, where the thresholds live. With it in, the game reduces to attrition-plus-grind, and attrition-plus-grind is exactly the game dispersion wins. Note the brief's own health table already flags this: mortar objective share is nearly double its <35% target. That row and the 71% row are the same problem.

4. **Twin nodes over-corrected — but in a specific, tunable way.** The AND condition and stranding were right and worked exactly as predicted. The mistake is that separation is *always* ≥3 of 8 columns. Stranding is therefore not a risk concentration runs — it is a certainty. A guaranteed punishment turns "don't over-concentrate" into "always maximize coverage." The scoring footprint (two nodes plus approaches, spanning most of the board's width, every single match) is constant, so the maximal-coverage shape is never wrong, and a pre-committed answer that is never wrong is what a dominant archetype *is*.

**What "punishing dispersion" can even mean here.** Be precise about this, because it rules out a whole class of fixes. In a static game with independent fire, a unit's value is fixed by its tile at commit. You can only punish a placement pattern two ways: kinetically (shot patterns that intersect it) or economically (tiles that turn out to be worthless). Kinetic anti-dispersion is a contradiction — dispersion is, by definition, the shape that minimizes intersection with every shot pattern; a weapon that hits everything punishes nothing, because a flat tax changes no decisions. So dispersion can only be punished *economically*: (a) make covered-but-nonscoring tiles worth ~0, (b) make scoring tiles require threshold mass to unlock, (c) make the coverage requirement *variable*, so pre-committed maximal coverage is sometimes over-insurance. All three are objective-structure levers. The missing piece is not a weapon and not a new rule of engagement — it is the value computation, and the cheapest handles on it are the two the game already has.

**Therefore, the fix, concretely — tune, don't add:**

- **Variable node separation (the brief's own suggestion; it is the right first move).** Per match, draw separation d uniformly from {2, 3, 4, 5}. Apply the *same d to both sides* — mirror the distance, never the columns; mirroring columns is what created the 80% shape, and distance-mirroring shares none of that mechanism (no shared lane both attacks and defends). Columns otherwise independent; keep the crater exclusion from node columns. At d=2, a three-column mass genuinely threatens both nodes and stranding stops being certain; at d=5, front line remains correct. The optimal shape becomes a function of the board draw, which means "dominant archetype" stops being a well-defined quantity — the exact property a placement game wants. Cost: one constant becomes a draw. This is the highest impact-per-complexity change available in the entire remaining space.

- **Mortar deals 30% damage to nodes (full damage to units).** This makes node damage lane-borne, which activates the sandbag thresholds and restores an economic reason to mass *somewhere*. It also directly fixes the flagged 63.9% metric. Why the §4 mortar measurements don't rule this out: shortening range 10→6 shuffled objective damage to the tank because the tank's node damage was equally formation-relevant to move around — but tank damage is lane-borne (needs LoS, needs an open lane), so damage shifting toward it is *healthy*, not neutral; and the splash/damage buff changed the mortar's magnitude, not the structural fact that indirect fire makes breakthroughs optional. Likewise "raise objective HP made rush stronger" was a single-HQ-era result that raised effective HP *against everyone*, giving the era's superior local DPS more time to work. A structure multiplier raises effective HP selectively against the one formation-independent damage source, in an era where stranding already caps what rush can gain. Different regime, different selectivity, not the same experiment.

Predicted combined effect: front line aggregate falls to the high 50s; conditional on d it splits — still ~68% at d=5, low 50s at d=2 against two-prong shapes. Watch timeouts (18.7% now; expect mid-20s, keep under 30%) and lane guard (if it exceeds ~58%, drop sandbags 90→75 as the release valve rather than touching the mortar again).

## 5.2 Was the wheel wrong, or incomplete?

Both, and I should own each half precisely.

**The AT Gun link was wrong, and predictably so from information I had.** I specified its value from a "96 per shot against an 8-deep stack" that the board cannot produce: the zone is 4 deep, and the brief's own §1 — in the *first* consult too — called the 4-deep zone load-bearing. Real deepest columns run 2.0–2.7, and the "stack" archetype turns out to be lateral clustering, not depth-stacking. Worse than merely missing its target, the AT Gun taxes the axis the geometry *already* taxes: `range − gap` punishes depth for free, so depth was the scarce axis before I added a weapon against it, while width — the abundant axis, the one the board hands out for free — went completely untaxed. A counter aimed at the scarce axis and nothing aimed at the abundant one is not an incomplete wheel; it is a wheel built facing backwards. The 12→22 damage change moving the dominant by one point confirms it: the unit is not under-tuned, it is mis-aimed, and no damage number fixes aim.

**The Stack ▸ Broad Front link wasn't disproven by the metagame — I severed it myself.** Rank 1 (AT Gun) assumed concentration would remain viable; rank 3 (twin nodes) was designed to kill concentration and did, completely. The wheel's first edge reversed because my own recommendation removed it. The weak 3-cycle underneath the top shape is the wheel's surviving remnant. To close the cycle you do not need a third hammer — you need to *restore the concentration ▸ dispersion edge economically*, which is exactly what the two 5.1 knobs do: at d=2–3 with lane-borne node damage, a massed two-and-a-half-column push can crack sandbag thresholds and double-threaten nodes, which a one-deep line cannot defend everywhere. The edge comes back as a *conditional* edge (dependent on the node draw and the opponent's guard), which is strictly better than the unconditional edge I originally specified.

**If a counter-weapon is ever still needed, the right axis is width, and this time the premise is board-verified.** The brief correctly warns that a unit linear in lane occupancy was tried and the board caps lanes at 4 (realized max ~2.7). But row occupancy caps at 8, and the current dominant *actually saturates it* — "spread across the full width of the front rank" means six to eight units in one row. That is the asymmetry that distinguishes this from the AT Gun mistake: the AT Gun's target shape never existed; a row-weapon's target shape is the measured 71% dominant. Verify before building: have the harness report mean row-adjacent enemy pairs for the front-line archetype (I expect 5–7); that number is to a row-weapon what the 2.74 was to the AT Gun, and this time it should come back large.

**But do not build a seventh unit.** The cheapest implementation is a retune of a piece already in the army: **MG enfilade** — MG shots deal full damage to the target and 50% to enemy units in the two laterally adjacent tiles (same row, ±1 column). Against a shoulder-to-shoulder line each burst hits three units (~2× effective output, from two MGs already on the board); against a spaced line it does nothing extra. Deterministic, previewable in the arc overlay, trivially debriefable ("your line stood shoulder-to-shoulder; their MGs raked it"), thematically exact. Critically, frame what this is: not a counter that beats front line, but curvature — it moves the optimum from "solid line" to "spaced line with deliberate gaps," and a spaced line pays an opportunity cost in uncovered lanes. That is the interior optimum this game has never had. If the AT Gun is still under 5% damage share after the 5.1 knobs land, replace it (not augment the roster) with a Rocket Battery — fires every ~12s at a pre-chosen 5-tile row segment for ~15 damage per occupied tile, value linear in row occupancy — but that is a contingency, not a plan.

## 5.3 Revised ranking

Ranked by impact per unit of complexity. Items 1–3 are one measurement cycle; the harness supports running them factorially (base / +separation / +mortar / both, 5,000 matches per cell).

**0. Fix the measurement first: LoS-aware generator + two new harness metrics.** Not a design change, but a prerequisite — half the remaining questions (idle, crater policy, whether front line is per-board beatable) are currently unanswerable because the generator parks units behind craters and the harness only measures archetype-vs-archetype. Add: (a) LoS-aware placement, (b) **tailoring gain** — fix a board, hill-climb single-piece edits for a best response, report the delta over generic front line and the number of edits needed. Metric (b) matters more than the archetype table from here on; see 5.5.

**1. Node-separation variance.** d ∈ {2,3,4,5} uniform, same d both sides, columns independent. As specified in 5.1. First, because it is one constant, it is the brief's own hypothesis, and it attacks the actual disease (a constant scoring footprint makes one pre-committed answer always right).

**2. Mortar structure multiplier, 0.30.** As specified in 5.1. Second, because it is one number, fixes an already-red health metric, and restores the concentration edge as a conditional edge.

**3. Debrief layer.** Promoted sharply, and it should ship before any further mechanics. Two reasons. The loop is edit-and-rerun; the debrief is the instrument that converts a loss into an edit, so it is the delivery mechanism for whatever counter-play the knobs create — counters that exist but can't be read might as well not exist. And it doubles as the design team's own diagnostic: two consult rounds have consisted of reconstructing *why* shapes win from aggregate tables; the debrief is that reconstruction, productized.

**4. MG enfilade (50% lateral splash).** Gated: build only if, after 1+2 are measured, the top shape still exceeds 60% and the adjacency-pair metric confirms solid-line adjacency is load-bearing. Specified in 5.2.

**5. Progressive reveal — demoted, deferred, possibly cut.** Yes, this was previously my recommendation; it has become a bad idea *in the current order*. The measured problem is that the optimum is opponent-independent — reveal only has value once tailoring matters, i.e., after knobs 1–2 create board- and opponent-dependence. It is also the most expensive item on the list in commit-flow and portrait-phone UX terms. Revisit only if, after the debrief ships, first-match blowout rates in real async duels show new players churning before their second run.

**6. Reserve wave — stays cut, but it is now the designated escalation, not a discard.** It is the highest-complexity item (touches the resolver, the UX, and mildly strains "reconstruct why you lost"). Do not spend it while one-line knobs are unmeasured. Its future role is specific: if post-knob measurement shows stranding punishes concentration too hard again, a pre-committed second wave (e.g., 12 pieces at t=0, 6 entering at t=20 at pre-chosen tiles — all decided before Ready, so the commitments hold) is the natural softener, and it happens to attack idle units and late-opening lanes as a side effect. It also roughly squares the placement space, which is 5.5's escalation path.

**Previously cut items that might now be necessary: none.** The §4 recomposition table is the evidence — every army composition variant landed at 70–78% dominant. Composition is not the binding axis; the value structure of placement is. Point-buy, roster growth, and synergy auras would multiply content while the effective dimensionality stayed where it is. Keep them cut. **Craters** are the one prior recommendation I'd partially walk back: their measured benefit (62.8→51.8) accrued to a shape twin nodes had already dethroned, and they cost tripled idle and a slower pace — but they also supply per-match board texture that serves the same goal as knob 1, so keep them with the 5.4 adjustments rather than reverting.

**Build first:** 0, then 1+2 factorially, then 3 while measuring. **Do not build:** a seventh unit, progressive reveal, the reserve wave, any further AT Gun tuning, anything from §4.

**Revised targets** (replacing "top archetype ≤55%"): no archetype >60% aggregate *and* none >65% conditional on any single d; median tailoring gain ≥25 points with median counter distance ≥4 edits; mortar objective share <35%; timeouts <30%; idle <12% measured with the LoS-aware generator.

## 5.4 Idle units

Decompose 22.8% into three parts before changing anything, because two of the parts aren't design problems.

- **Generator artifact (probably half or more).** The generator places without LoS checks; a human with the arc-preview overlay will not park a soldier behind a crater. The figure tripled exactly when craters landed, which is the signature of blind placement colliding with new blockers, not of players being starved of agency. Re-measure with the LoS-aware generator; my expectation is 22.8% falls to the mid-teens on that change alone.
- **Late-opening lanes under the 45s cap (real, small).** The proper eventual fix is the reserve wave, not crater surgery. Live with it for now.
- **Deep/turtle placements out of range (archetype artifact).** Turtle idles by construction; that is the archetype being bad, which is fine.

**Crater changes now:** cap at exactly 2 per side (drop the 3-crater draws — the pace metric degraded from 40.1% to 27.8% mid-length matches after craters, and the third crater is the marginal one), and add "no two craters in the same or adjacent columns" so a draw can never wall off a board quadrant. Keep the mirror, keep the node-column and rear-rank exclusions.

**Crater changes only if needed:** if idle is still >15% with the LoS-aware generator, convert craters from hard LoS blockers to deterministic soft cover — shots crossing a crater deal 50% damage. That preserves their concentration tax and their board-texture value while making hard idleness impossible, stays fully deterministic, and reads cleanly in the debrief. Do not do this preemptively; hard blockers are more legible, and legibility is a commitment.

What I would *not* do: remove craters. Per-match terrain variance is one of only two sources of board-dependence in the game (the other being node draws), and 5.1's whole argument is that board-dependence is what dissolves dominant archetypes.

## 5.5 The honest question

**The decision space is not too small in raw terms — it is too small in effective dimensions, and the 71% is not actually the alarming number.** Eighteen pieces on 32 tiles is an astronomical placement space. But all of it currently projects onto about two dials — how spread, how forward — and both dials have monotone optima, so the astronomical space collapses to one right answer. The alarming number is not 71% against the field; it is the clean head-to-head sweep (73/73/70/60/93) combined with two consecutive eras of monotone dominance. Together those say the optimum is *opponent-independent*: the best placement barely depends on what the enemy did. That is the finding that threatens this specific game, because the entire premise of edit-and-rerun is "read their board, tailor a counter." A dominant archetype is survivable; an opponent-independent optimum means there is nothing to tailor, and the edit loop converges to the same answer every session. The disease would kill the real loop, not just the ladder stat.

**So split the question in two, and answer each plainly:**

*Is ~70% acceptable for an edit-and-rerun game?* Yes — conditionally. For a game whose loop is iterating against one specific committed board, population win rates are close to irrelevant *provided* per-board counters exist and are findable. The right acceptance test is the tailoring-gain metric from 5.3: fix a front-line board, search placements for a best response. If a tailored answer beats that specific board by 25+ points and sits 4+ edits away from generic front line, the game is healthy at 70%-vs-the-field, full stop — a shape can be the best opening bid and still lose every rematch to a player who reads the board. In that world, stop chasing a flat archetype table entirely; it is the wrong instrument for this game. Effort goes to the debrief, board variety (node draws, craters), and the async duel experience — the things that make per-board counter-discovery fast and satisfying.

*Is the space too small?* On today's ruleset, my honest bet is that the tailoring test **fails** — the 5.1 diagnosis says value is concave in spread and forward-pegged *regardless of the opponent's layout*, so generic front line is probably within an edit or two of the best response to almost every board. If the measurement confirms that, then yes, the space is too small along its one effective axis — but the smallest sufficient enlargement is not more pieces, tiles, or shots at placement. It is *coupling*: per-match objective variance couples your shape to the board (knob 1); lane-borne node damage couples your shape to the opponent's defense (knob 2); enfilade couples your spacing to the opponent's weapon placement (item 4). Three couplings, two of them single-number changes, zero new content. That is the whole bet, and it is cheap enough to measure in one harness cycle.

**And if the bet fails** — if after knobs 1+2 (and enfilade if gated in) the top shape still clears 60% with low tailoring gain — then the verdict is that a single simultaneous placement genuinely cannot support this metagame, and the smallest structural enlargement is the reserve wave: split the 18 pieces into a t=0 deployment and a pre-committed t=20 wave. That adds a real time dimension, roughly squares the effective decision space, softens AND-condition stranding, and attacks idle units, while preserving every hard commitment (no movement, no RNG, all decisions before Ready, reconstructable losses). It is the one enlargement worth its complexity, and it should be bought last, only after the one-line knobs have been given their chance to prove the space was big enough all along.
